This dissertation asks what it means for scientific practice to hold a universe open to wonder. It examines the search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) as a place where the work of science quietly does the work of religion — mediating presence and absence, cultivating openness to distant, transcendent others, and managing the kind of radical uncertainty that ordinary life tends to close down. Rather than treating science and religion as opposing accounts of the world, the project argues that SETI researchers are engaged in a form of religious labor, even as they insist on staying strictly secular.

Drawing on fieldwork inside SETI's research culture, the dissertation traces two intertwined practices that sustain this openness. Infrastructural practices — radio observatories, signal-processing algorithms, institutional collaborations — build the material conditions under which an alien signal could ever become detectable. Promissory practices — the stories researchers tell about future contact, the experiments designed around it — orient that infrastructure toward what might still happen. Together, these form what Connor calls *pre-enchantment*: not a return to older, pre-modern forms of mystery, but an enchantment produced by scientific practice itself.
